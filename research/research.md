# Edge Multi-Model Batch Scheduling: Annotated Bibliography

Companion to "Edge Multi-Model Batch Scheduling on a Single Accelerator: Prior Art, Adjacent Techniques and Baselines". Works are grouped by category. Each entry gives the citation, a real link, and a deep comparison against our own problem.

Our problem, for reference: N models with their own queues, one edge accelerator, one batch at a time, non-preemptive, measured per-model T_i(b), routing fixed. Objectives are mean turnaround and busy time.

---

## 1. Closest prior work (same system model)

**EdgeServing: Deadline-Aware Multi-DNN Serving at the Edge.** J. Cao, X. Li, Q. Liu, T. Han, N. Zhang, W. Shi. arXiv:2605.05527 [cs.DC], May 2026. Accepted at IEEE ICCCN 2026 according to the arXiv comment.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05527

Cost model is measured, not assumed: offline profiles every (model, exit-point, batch-size) combination on the target GPU and stores P95 latency in a lookup table L(m,e,B); latency grows sub-linearly with batch size (10x batch, only ~2-3x latency). Scheduler is a one-step greedy heuristic — at each free slot it looks at all queues' lengths and per-task waiting times, picks each queue's max feasible batch B*=min(|Q|,B_max), the deepest exit satisfying the SLO, then dispatches the queue whose "stability score" (an exponential urgency function over predicted future waits) shows the least global SLO damage. This is the closest of every paper here to our "pick a queue and batch size from a measured curve" setup, and it also enforces one queue per batch with no spatial sharing (time-division only, one batch exclusive at a time — matches us). Validated on RTX 3080, GTX 1650, and Jetson Orin Nano. **Sharpest difference:** it adds a model/exit-depth choice per job (accuracy-latency tradeoff), turning scheduling into a 3D (model, exit, batch) decision, and its objective is SLO-violation-rate, not mean turnaround or total compute — whereas our problem fixes the model per job and searches only over (queue, batch size) to minimize turnaround + compute.

## 2. Edge multi-DNN systems with concurrent or spatial GPU sharing

**BCEdge: SLO-Aware DNN Inference Services with Adaptive Batching on Edge Platforms.** arXiv:2305.01519; IEEE Transactions on Network and Service Management, 2024, DOI 10.1109/TNSM.2024.3409701.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01519

Also measures rather than assumes cost: a runtime profiler periodically collects throughput/latency for each (batch size, concurrency-level) pair per model. Mechanism is maximum-entropy deep RL: state = (model type, input shape, SLO, resources, queue info), action = a discrete (batch size b, number of concurrent model instances) pair, reward = a scalarized utility U = log(throughput/latency / (ΣSLO/instances)). Crucially, its action space includes running multiple concurrent instances of models simultaneously — exactly the kind of concurrent multi-batch execution our single-accelerator, one-batch-at-a-time constraint forbids. Validated on Jetson Xavier NX (primary), Nano and TX2, across YOLOv5/MobileNetV3/ResNet-18/EfficientNet-B0/Inception-v3/TinyBERT. **Sharpest difference:** treats "how many models run concurrently" as a first-class scheduling variable, whereas we assume strict one-batch-at-a-time, no-concurrency execution.

**SEEB-GPU: Early-Exit Aware Scheduling and Batching for Edge GPU Inference.** Subramaniyan, Joshi, Wang, Brocanelli. ACM/IEEE Symposium on Edge Computing (SEC) 2025, DOI 10.1145/3769102.3772715.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3769102.3772715

Cost model is a closed-form linear regression fit to offline profiling: L = α + β1·s + β2·θ + β3/|TPCs| — latency as an affine function of batch size s, confidence threshold θ, and inverse allocated GPU compute-cluster count (closer to an assumed parametric form than our measured, possibly convex, per-model curve, though the coefficients themselves are data-fit). Scheduler enumerates power-of-two batch sizes, filters by deadline-minus-slack, picks the one minimizing C = λ1·L − λ2·θ. Exploits spatial GPU sharing via TPC (thread-processing-cluster) masking — multiple DNN tasks run concurrently on disjoint SM subsets of the same GPU. Validated on RTX 3090 (41 TPCs) with ResNet50/34, MobileNetV2, ShuffleNet. **Sharpest difference:** schedules by spatially partitioning a single GPU across simultaneously-running batches from different models, directly violating our "one batch from one queue at a time, non-preemptive" accelerator model.

## 3. Preemption and early-exit approaches

**Pantheon: Preemptible Multi-DNN Inference on Mobile Edge GPUs.** L. Han, Z. Zhou, Z. Li. ACM MobiSys 2024, DOI 10.1145/3643832.3661878. (Not the CMU networking Pantheon.)
**Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3643832.3661878

Cost model is empirical/profiled: per-variant accuracy is profiled offline, execution time computed chunk-by-chunk from how much of a DNN remains after preemption (measured preemption overhead only 46-219 microseconds). Mechanism is deadline-driven: tasks sorted earliest-deadline-first, and if a task would miss its deadline the scheduler preempts whichever running task has the "least accuracy drop" if downgraded, mid-execution, at chunk (layer-group) granularity, via two-tier CUDA stream priorities. Objective: maximize aggregate accuracy across tasks subject to per-task deadlines. Validated on Jetson Xavier NX and Nano, plus a field deployment on an autonomous vehicle prototype. **Sharpest difference:** the entire contribution is fine-grained mid-inference preemption and per-task DNN-variant switching — both explicitly disallowed in our problem (non-preemptive, model per job fixed upstream, batch runs to completion).

**Fluid Batching: Exit-Aware Preemptive Serving of Early-Exit Neural Networks on Edge NPUs.** A. Kouris, S. I. Venieris, S. Laskaridis, N. D. Lane. arXiv:2209.13443; ICCAD 2023 (Best Paper Award finalist).
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13443

Cost model is a measured lookup table: per-exit latency swept over batch sizes 1..B_max at design time, stored in an N_exits × B_max LUT. Scheduling rule is a "batch-refill" mechanism: dispatches immediately on arrival, and at each early-exit checkpoint computes remaining active batch slots and admits newly-queued samples up to that slack, but only if the overhead is smaller than remaining SLO slack. Objective: maximize weighted throughput across batch sizes. Preemption is checkpoint-level (only at early-exit boundaries) and allows out-of-order completion and mid-execution batch composition changes — both disallowed in our model — though, like us, only one batch executes at a time (no spatial concurrency). Requires custom NPU hardware (FPGA prototypes: Xilinx ZC706, ZCU104); not deployable on a stock Jetson. **Sharpest difference:** the whole mechanism is built around checkpoint-level preemption to dynamically resize/merge in-flight batches, which our "batch runs to completion once dispatched" constraint rules out entirely.

## 4. Real-time and deadline-driven edge scheduling

**DeepRT: A Soft Real Time Scheduler for Computer Vision Applications on the Edge.** Z. Yang, K. Nahrstedt, H. Guo, Q. Zhou. ACM/IEEE SEC 2021; arXiv:2105.01803.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.01803

Cost model splits latency into queuing delay plus execution time, and execution time is *measured* per (model, batch size) via offline profiling with NVIDIA Triton, stored as lookup tables — like us, batch cost is size-dependent and empirical, not assumed constant. Mechanism (DisBatcher) is fixed-length time-windowing per request category: divides time into windows of length half the smallest deadline in that category, and merges same-model frames arriving within a window into one batch job — batch size is a side effect of window width and arrival rate, not chosen by an optimizer at dispatch time. Runs job instances one at a time, non-preemptively, ordered by EDF across per-category queues (structurally similar to our N-model setup). Objective is purely deadline-centric (maximize admitted-and-met soft deadlines); no compute/energy minimization. Validated on desktop RTX 2080 and Jetson TX2. **Sharpest difference:** DeepRT never searches over batch size b as a decision variable — b is a derived consequence of a fixed time-window policy, whereas our scheduler explicitly picks b ≤ |Q_i| per dispatch to trade off turnaround against compute.

**EdgeBatch: Towards AI-Empowered Optimal Task Batching in Intelligent Edge Systems.** D. Zhang, N. Vance, Y. Zhang, M. T. Rashid, D. Wang. IEEE RTSS 2019, DOI 10.1109/RTSS46320.2019.00040.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1109/RTSS46320.2019.00040

Cost model is explicitly size-dependent and empirically measured: profiles per-node functions f(|B|) (delay) and g(|B|) (power) as functions of batch size — the same "bigger batch lowers per-item compute but eventually hurts latency" shape our own data shows. Mechanism is an online learning algorithm (extended PROD/regret-minimization) maintaining a weight vector over candidate batch sizes per node, updating toward the batch size that minimizes a combined delay+energy cost in hindsight — the "state" it tracks is recent regret across batch-size choices, not queue occupancy directly. Objective: minimize λ·delay + (1−λ)·energy subject to a deadline threshold. Validated on a real heterogeneous testbed (Jetson TX1/TX2/TK1, Raspberry Pi 3 cluster) with actual current-sensor energy measurement — but "batching" here is homogeneous, one task type distributed across many physical *devices*, not multiple distinct models sharing one accelerator. **Sharpest difference:** optimizes batch size for a single task type spread across multiple devices, never facing our core constraint of choosing which of several queues (different models, different T_i curves) to serve next on one shared accelerator.

**RT-mDL: Supporting Real-Time Mixed Deep Learning Tasks on Edge Platforms.** N. Ling, K. Wang, Y. He, G. Xing, D. Xie. ACM SenSys 2021, DOI 10.1145/3485730.3485938.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3485730.3485938

Cost model is size-dependent and measured empirically across platforms (Xavier, TX2, desktop), plus profiles an accuracy/latency trade-off per model variant. Mechanism splits each task into three subtasks (CPU preprocess, GPU inference, CPU postprocess) from separate priority queues; the novel piece is GPU task packing — two priority-tiered CUDA streams into which a lookahead scheduler searches for compatible ready tasks to co-locate *concurrently* under a GPU utilization constraint, rather than picking a batch of same-type jobs. Objective is explicitly multi-dimensional: minimize deadline-miss rate subject to a cap, jointly with a normalized accuracy-loss term from selecting lower-accuracy variants. Validated on Jetson TX2/Nano/AGX Xavier plus desktop, demonstrated live on an F1/10 autonomous vehicle. **Sharpest difference:** "packing" is about scheduling multiple *different* tasks to run concurrently (time/stream-sliced) rather than grouping same-model jobs into a batch of size b — it never performs the batch-size selection b ≤ |Q_i| that is our central decision variable.

## 5. Surveys

**Scheduling Techniques of AI Models on Modern Heterogeneous Edge GPU: A Critical Review.** arXiv:2506.01377.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01377

Scope is layer-level and cross-accelerator task scheduling (partitioning/placing DNN layers across a Jetson's GPU, DLA, PVA, VIC, CPU, and running multiple concurrent DNN instances such as HaX-CoNN — reviews ten Jetson schedulers: Jedi, CP-CNN, Herald, H2H, LP, AxoNN, GA, HaX-CoNN, Map-and-Conquer, MaGNAS). It does not address batch-size selection, per-model measured batching cost curves, or cross-queue scheduling for multiple model types sharing an accelerator's batch dispatch. **Sharpest difference:** confirms our specific problem — choosing when/what batch size to run across N queues on one shared accelerator using measured T_i(b) curves — is outside its coverage, supporting a "this gap is unexplored" citation.

## 6. Batch-service queueing theory and batching control

**A General Class of Bulk Queues with Poisson Input.** M. F. Neuts. Annals of Mathematical Statistics 38(3), 1967, pp. 759-770.
**Link:** https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.aoms/1177698869

Introduces the general "bulk service rule" (a,b) class: the server waits until at least `a` customers are present, then serves a batch of size between `a` and `b`, with i.i.d. service times from a single fixed distribution independent of which jobs actually rode in the batch. Core result is a transform/generating-function solution for the queue-length Markov chain — foundational abstraction that essentially every later batch-service paper below specializes. **Sharpest difference:** service time is batch-size-*independent* and single-class, single-queue, with no queue-selection decision at all — our problem breaks every specializing assumption (N heterogeneous queues, a per-model measured non-constant T_i(b), and an active scheduler choice of b and which queue to serve). Transfers only as vocabulary/methodology: the (a,b)-rule formalism and transform-based analysis are reusable tools for computing turnaround statistics once a policy is fixed, not a source of an optimal policy for our setting.

**Optimal Control of Parallel Queues with Batch Service.** C. H. Xia, G. Michailidis, N. Bambos, P. W. Glynn. Probability in the Engineering and Informational Sciences 16(3):289-307, 2002.
**Link:** https://web.stanford.edu/~glynn/papers/2002/XiaMichailidisBambosG02.pdf

Closest structural match to our topology (parallel queues, one batch server, per-queue routing fixed), but proves optimality only under heavy symmetry: i.i.d. **exponential** service times **independent of batch size**, mutually independent Poisson arrivals with **equal rates**, no cross-class batching. Under these, Longest-Queue-first (LQ) stochastically maximizes throughput (infinite buffers) and Fewest-Empty-Space minimizes loss (finite equal buffers); unequal buffers only get simulation-identified heuristics, not a proof. **Sharpest difference:** every load-bearing symmetry fails for us — T_i(b) is model-specific and size-dependent (one near-affine, one convex) rather than a constant service time, and their proof technique (stochastic coupling across exchangeable parallel queues) has no obvious generalization once queue index carries information about the cost function. The LQ/FES proof *strategy* (sample-path stochastic dominance via coupling) is a useful template for what a proof in our setting would need to control for; the LQ policy itself has no optimality claim once costs are heterogeneous and convex in b.

**Optimal Control for Parallel Queues with a Single Batch Server.** Chen and Wang. Operations Research Letters 50(4):377-383, 2022, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2022.04.004.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.orl.2022.04.004 (SSRN preprint: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4082841)

Confirmed abstract: "multiple Poisson arrival queues and a single batch server that has infinite capacity and a fixed service time" — like Xia et al., batch service time is a single constant, independent of batch size or class, base model infinite-buffer. **Unresolved:** could not get past the ScienceDirect paywall or the rate-limited SSRN full text to confirm whether the finite-capacity extension is a full optimality proof or just noted as "a natural extension" — treat the finite-buffer claim as unconfirmed until the full Section 4/5 text is checked. Either way, the headline result breaks on contact with our problem for the same reason as Xia et al.: fixed, size- and class-independent service time is the load-bearing assumption, and T_i(b) is neither. Transfers only as a structural template (single server, parallel queues, batch-size decision), not a usable optimal policy.

**Assumption comparison — our model vs. the two closest OR papers:**

**Optimal Control of Batch Service Queues.** R. K. Deb, R. F. Serfozo. Advances in Applied Probability 5(2), 1973.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.2307/1426040 — plus follow-ups: Deb (1976), "Optimal control of batch service queues with switching costs," Adv. Appl. Prob. 8(1):177-194 (https://doi.org/10.2307/1426531-style Cambridge Core listing); Zeng & Xia (2017), "Optimal bulking threshold of batch service queues," J. Appl. Prob. 54(2):409-423, DOI 10.1017/jpr.2017.8.

Deb & Serfozo prove that for a **single** batch-service queue (fixed capacity Q, service+holding cost MDP), the optimal policy is a **single threshold M**: serve min(x,Q) iff queue length x ≥ M, else wait. Deb (1976) extends this to a controllable on/off server with switching costs, proving a **two-threshold (hysteresis)** policy — start at M, stop at m ≤ M — is optimal. Zeng & Xia (2017) instead study the M/G^[a,b]/1/N bulk-service queue's steady-state performance and derive a polynomial algorithm for the minimum bulking threshold that minimizes expected waiting time, given a fixed max batch cap. All three share load-bearing assumptions that break for us: a **single** queue/server (no routing/selection across N models), and a batch service time either **fixed constant per batch** (Deb-Serfozo, Deb 1976) or dependent on batch size only through a simple parametric family (Zeng-Xia), never an arbitrary measured curve where one model is affine and another convex. None address compute-cost minimization as an objective. **Transferable idea:** the threshold/hysteresis structure itself is a strong candidate for the qualitative shape of an optimal per-queue batching rule in our problem, even though the exact threshold value needs re-deriving against convex/affine T_i(b) rather than an assumed constant cost.

**A Stochastic Batching and Scheduling Problem.** J. Koole, R. Righter. Probability in the Engineering and Informational Sciences 15:465-479, 2001.
**Link:** https://www.math.vu.nl/~koole/publications/2001peis3/art.pdf

Batch's processing time is the **max** of its members' individual (random) processing times — a semiconductor-burn-in model — and explicitly relaxes the "all jobs identical" assumption to allow heterogeneous per-job distributions. Sharpest result restricted to **exponential** processing times with **max batch size 2**: a polynomial algorithm for the flowtime-optimal policy, plus the counterintuitive finding that grouping same-distribution jobs together isn't always optimal. Architecturally close to us (heterogeneous classes, batch-size effect on service time) but diverges in mechanism: their duration is a max-statistic over independent per-job random times (emergent from batch composition), whereas our T_i(b) is a deterministic, externally measured function of b alone, and our batch sizes go well beyond 2. Their heterogeneity is also across jobs *within a shared queue*, not across parallel queues each feeding one model. **Transferable lesson:** stochastic batch-scheduling intuition can invert relative to deterministic scheduling — useful caution when designing heuristics for our convex-T_i(b) queues — but the exact algorithm doesn't port (tied to exponential service and pairwise batching).

**Reference multi-class control baselines.**
**Link:** cμ-rule — Buyukkoc, Varaiya & Walrand, "The cμ Rule Revisited," Advances in Applied Probability 17(1):237-238, 1985: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-applied-probability/article/c-rule-revisited/4208A5791793438658BF774D4D9DF013 · **Citation correction:** the "polling systems with set-up costs (Koole, Queueing Systems, 1998, DOI 10.1007/BF01151932)" reference in the earlier draft was wrong — that DOI/title actually resolves to **Duenyas & Van Oyen, "Stochastic Scheduling of Parallel Queues with Set-up Costs," Queueing Systems 19(4):421-444, 1995** (confirmed via Springer + Semantic Scholar). Koole does have a topically adjacent 1997 paper, "Assigning a Single Server to Inhomogeneous Queues with Switching Costs" (venue unconfirmed) — flagged, not yet verified as the right substitute.

The cμ-rule proves that for a **single non-batching** server, multiple queues with linear holding costs c_i and exponential class-specific service rates μ_i, serving whichever queue has the largest c_i·μ_i minimizes expected holding cost — a per-job, one-at-a-time index rule with no batching at all. Duenyas & Van Oyen add non-preemptive set-up costs/times between queues and characterize a hysteresis-like switching policy. **Sharpest difference:** neither contemplates a batch-size decision, since there's no scalar μ_i to rank by once service cost is a size-dependent curve T_i(b). **Transferable use:** cμ becomes a sanity-check baseline at b=1 (our problem literally reduces to single-job multiclass queueing there), and the set-up-cost switching-hysteresis structure is a reasonable prior for how a real scheduler should resist thrashing between queues.

**Whittle-index / restless-bandit treatments of multi-class queueing.**
**Link:** Glazebrook, Lumley & Ansell, "Index Heuristics for Multiclass M/G/1 Systems with Nonpreemptive Service and Convex Holding Costs," Queueing Systems 45(2), 2003: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1026060405346 · Larrañaga, Ayesta & Verloop, "Index Policies for a Multi-Class Queue with Convex Holding Cost and Abandonments," ACM SIGMETRICS 2014: https://www.irit.fr/~Urtzi.Ayesta/argi/sigmetrics14.pdf · **Citation correction:** José Niño-Mora is not a confirmed co-author on the Glazebrook paper — the earlier "Ansell, Glazebrook & Niño-Mora" attribution should read Glazebrook, Lumley & Ansell (2003).

Both use a Whittle/Lagrangian relaxation of a **single non-preemptive multiclass queue** with **convex holding costs** to build index heuristics; Larrañaga et al. add exponential service/Poisson arrivals/abandonment and prove the resulting index policy is asymptotically optimal as load grows (exactly optimal only in the linear-cost/cμ special case). Both are single-job (not batch) service, decoupling queues via a Lagrange multiplier on a shared resource constraint — structurally closer to our "shared single accelerator, N queues" setup than most papers here. **Sharpest difference:** the object being relaxed is a per-job service decision, not a batch-size decision with a size-dependent cost curve; their convexity lives in *holding cost as a function of queue length*, not in *T_i(b) as a function of batch size*. **Transferable idea:** restless-bandit/Whittle relaxation is a natural way to decouple our N queues by giving each a per-queue index trading waiting cost against T_i(b) compute cost — re-deriving an index under a batch-size decision variable is a plausible, genuinely novel extension worth attempting.

**Queueing Analysis of GPU-Based Inference Servers with Dynamic Batching: A Closed-Form Characterization.** Y. Inoue. arXiv:1912.06322; Performance Evaluation vol. 147, 2021.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06322

Models a single-server, single-queue batch-service system, infinite buffer, one job class only — no multiple model types or queues. Batch-cost function is assumed **linear** in batch size (a simplification defended as characteristic of GPU inference, not a measured, model-specific curve — no affine-vs-convex distinction). Control policy is fixed and non-adaptive: whenever the server idles and the queue is non-empty, it immediately batches *all* waiting jobs — no batch-size decision variable, no thresholding, no wait-vs-serve choice. Objective is purely analytical: closed-form mean queue length/latency, showing energy efficiency rises with arrival rate; validated against latency curves on a real GPU server. **Sharpest difference:** no scheduling decision at all (batch-everything-now is fixed, not optimized) and no cross-class competition — our problem is precisely about *choosing* b and *which* queue when N heterogeneous, differently-shaped T_i(b) curves compete for one non-preemptive resource.

**SMDP-Based Dynamic Batching for Efficient Inference on GPU-Based Platforms.** Xu, Sun, Zhou, Niu. arXiv:2301.12865 (extended journal version: arXiv:2501.02181, "...for Improving Responsiveness and Energy Efficiency of Batch Services" — same authors, adds a tail-state abstraction cutting state-space/computation cost by ~63.5%/98%, not a different model).
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12865

Single-queue, single-GPU, single-model system: one Poisson stream, one accelerator, no competing model classes. Batch-cost function is explicitly linear/affine and **assumed**, not measured per-model: τ(b) = αb + τ0 for latency and a parallel affine form for energy, justified only by a generic "GPU inference latency scales linearly" claim. Control policy is a genuine SMDP with action space {wait, serve at size 1..B_max}, solved via discretized dynamic programming; the optimal policy is shown numerically to have a control-limit (threshold) structure, with the threshold rising monotonically with the weight on energy vs. latency. Objective: minimize weighted mean response time + power, evaluated only via simulation, no physical hardware. **Sharpest difference:** the threshold policy is derived for one job class with one known affine cost curve, so it has nothing to say about *which* queue to serve, let alone a genuinely measured, possibly convex T_i(b) — our setting needs a joint queue-selection-and-batch-size policy across N differently-shaped, empirically-measured cost functions.

**Joint Batching and Scheduling (JBAS) for High-Throughput Multiuser Edge AI with Asynchronous Task Arrivals.** Cang, Chen, Huang. arXiv:2307.14350.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14350

K devices offload tasks to a single edge server running one shared, pre-trained model (e.g. a single ResNet-50) — despite "multiuser," exactly one model class; heterogeneity comes from asynchronous arrival times and per-task deadlines, not different models with different cost curves. Batch-cost function is linear/affine, d(b) = a·b + b0, empirically validated on a Jetson TX1 running ResNet-50 — a real measurement, but of a single affine curve, never convex. Jointly optimizes task-to-batch assignment, batch sizes, and radio/uplink allocation to maximize throughput subject to spectrum constraints, solved via convex relaxation plus alternating optimization; simulation only, no physical batching hardware test. **Sharpest difference:** JBAS's "batching" problem is fundamentally a task-grouping and wireless-resource-allocation problem for one model; our problem has no radio dimension and instead chooses among N queues each running a *different* model with its own independently measured, non-affine T_i(b) curve on a shared accelerator.

## 7. Incompatible job families / batch processing machines

**Scheduling Batch Processing Machines with Incompatible Job Families.** R. Uzsoy. International Journal of Production Research 33(10), 1995, pp. 2685-2708.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1080/00207549508904839

**Citation note:** there are two distinct Uzsoy IJPR papers from this era often conflated — this one (incompatible families, IJPR 33(10), 1995) and a separate paper, "Scheduling a Single Batch Processing Machine with Non-Identical Job Sizes" (IJPR 32(7), 1994, DOI 10.1080/00207549408957026), which is about arbitrary job *sizes within one family*, not incompatible families. Cite the right one.

Purely deterministic/offline scheduling: a static job set, known processing times per family, machine capacity B, minimizing makespan (and a companion max-lateness result) via dynamic programming and heuristics — not a queue with arrivals. Batch cost is constant regardless of composition (processing time = the family's fixed job time, independent of how many jobs of size ≤ B are packed in), with a modest fixed number of families (small enough for DP state space). **Sharpest parallel:** the family-partitioned structure itself — origin of "incompatible job families on a batch machine," exactly our one-class-per-batch constraint. **Sharpest gap:** offline (all jobs known up front, no queueing dynamics or arrivals) and constant, size-independent per-batch cost — the entire "how big should a batch be" tradeoff that drives our scheduler doesn't exist here.

**Stochastic Scheduling of a Batch Processing Machine with Incompatible Job Families.** I. Duenyas, W. Neale. Annals of Operations Research 70:191-220, 1997.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1018922104670

Genuinely a stochastic queueing model, the closest of this whole cluster to our online setting: jobs from incompatible families arrive as random processes, processing times random, holding costs accrue per unit waiting time, objective is long-run average cost. Proceeds in three stages: (1) optimal policy for the static/clearing case as a full-information DP; (2) for dynamic arrivals under a no-idling constraint, shows the optimal policy is **control-limit (threshold) type** — start serving a family once its queue reaches a threshold, echoing Deb-Serfozo-style results extended to multiple incompatible classes; (3) a simple index/threshold heuristic validated by simulation since the exact optimal policy for the unrestricted (idling-allowed) case is intractable. Exact threshold-value characterization was paywalled — worth verifying against the full PDF if the precise functional form matters. Batch service cost is effectively constant (fixed distribution per family, independent of batch size beyond a capacity cap), small number of families (2 in their illustration). **Sharpest parallel:** the only paper here with genuine queueing dynamics and a "which class + when to cut a batch" decision under random arrivals — functionally identical in spirit to our scheduler's job. **Sharpest gap:** service-time randomness is exogenous per-batch noise around a fixed mean, not a *deterministic, measured, batch-size-dependent* curve the scheduler can exploit for compute-cost tradeoffs; they optimize holding cost rather than turnaround+compute jointly and don't model batch service time that structurally grows with b.

**Control of Manufacturing Networks Which Contain a Batch Processing Machine.** J.J. Neale, I. Duenyas. IIE Transactions 32, 2000, pp. 1027-1041.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1013780307222

Natural sequel to the 1997 paper — still stochastic queueing/control, but the batch machine is now embedded in a multi-stage network (upstream/downstream stations feed/drain it). Focus shifts to how the control-limit policy interacts with the network (starving downstream, blocking upstream); objective becomes network-level throughput/flow-time. Small fixed number of families, batch service time roughly fixed per family. **Sharpest parallel:** shows the control-limit idea is robust to network context, not just a toy single-queue result. **Sharpest gap:** about network topology and station interaction, irrelevant to our single-accelerator setup with fixed external routing and no upstream/downstream blocking; still assumes constant per-batch cost — furthest of the group from our compute-cost objective since it doesn't optimize compute at all.

**Minimizing Total Tardiness on a Batch Processing Machine with Incompatible Job Families.** S.V. Mehta, R. Uzsoy. IIE Transactions 30, 1998, pp. 165-178.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007466101115

Deterministic/offline scheduling, motivated by semiconductor burn-in ovens: static job set, incompatible families, and — notably — within a family all jobs share an identical, fixed processing time (the strongest version of "constant batch cost" in this group). Pseudo-polynomial/polynomial DP for total tardiness with fixed families/capacity, plus heuristics for larger instances; illustrated with 2-4 families. **Sharpest parallel:** the family-based structure and tardiness-style objective is conceptually close to our "minimize mean turnaround" goal, and the DP state (family, jobs batched, elapsed time) resembles a scheduler enumerating (queue, batch-size) choices. **Sharpest gap:** fully offline with release/due dates known a priori, and the same-processing-time-within-family assumption is the opposite of our core empirical fact that T_i(b) is a nontrivial function of b — no "should I batch bigger" tradeoff exists in their cost model at all.

**Minimizing Total Weighted Tardiness on a Single Batch Process Machine with Incompatible Job Families.** I.C. Perez, J.W. Fowler, W.M. Carlyle. Computers & Operations Research 32(2), 2005, pp. 327-341.
**Link:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305054803002399

Deterministic/offline, semiconductor diffusion/oxidation furnace context; static job list, known due dates, weighted-tardiness objective. NP-hard even for one machine, so decomposed into batch formation then batch sequencing, each solved with heuristics rather than an exact algorithm; batch processing time fixed per batch (typically the max, or a fixed furnace-cycle time), independent of how many lots are packed in below capacity. **Sharpest parallel:** the two-stage decomposition (decide grouping, then order/timing) is structurally close to how a real scheduler for our problem might be built — pick a queue, pick a batch size, then decide when to fire it. **Sharpest gap:** static/offline job list known in advance, constant-cost-per-batch clashes with our measured T_i(b), and there's no notion of minimizing total compute — their heuristics never trade "run now with fewer jobs" against "wait and batch bigger to save compute."

**Heuristic Scheduling of Jobs on Parallel Batch Machines with Incompatible Job Families and Unequal Ready Times.** L. Mönch, H. Balasubramanian, J.W. Fowler, M.E. Pfund. Computers & Operations Research 32(11), 2005, pp. 2731-2750.
**Link:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305054804000759

Chosen as the representative Mönch et al. paper — most-cited in his "parallel batch machines + incompatible families + GA heuristics" line, explicitly uses a genetic algorithm as one decomposition strategy. Deterministic/offline (jobs have known ready times rather than a live arrival process), targeting total weighted tardiness across *multiple parallel* batch machines rather than a single accelerator; batch cost per family fixed. **Sharpest parallel:** closest paper in this set to combinatorial-search heuristics (GA-based) for jointly deciding grouping AND machine/timing assignment, conceptually adjacent to any search-based heuristic one might design for our batching decision. **Sharpest gap:** multi-machine rather than single-accelerator, offline with ready-times known in advance rather than online periodic arrivals, and "batch size vs. runtime" simply isn't a design variable in its cost model the way it is in ours.

## 8. General multi-model DNN serving (datacenter-scale, non-edge)

**Clipper: A Low-Latency Online Prediction Serving System.** D. Crankshaw et al. NSDI 2017.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi17/technical-sessions/presentation/crankshaw (preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03079)

Sits between trained models and applications: a Model Abstraction Layer with per-model prediction caches and generic adaptive batching that greedily increases batch size while predicted (extrapolated) latency stays under an SLO — cost is implicitly size-dependent, but purely as a latency-target knob, not an objective to minimize. Multi-model in that it serves many independently-containerized models behind one frontend, but each model's batching decision is made independently, with no cross-queue/cross-accelerator arbitration. Target hardware is a general serving cluster (CPU/GPU containers, horizontal scaling); headline objective is tail-latency SLO compliance with best-effort accuracy. **Sharpest difference:** never solves "one accelerator, N queues, pick queue+batch size" — assumes each model effectively gets its own execution resource and only tunes that model's own batch size against a latency target.

**Nexus: A GPU Cluster Engine for Accelerating DNN-Based Video Analysis.** H. Shen et al. SOSP 2019.
**Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3341301.3359658 (PDF: https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~arvind/papers/nexus.pdf)

Core mechanism is squishy bin-packing plus batch-aware scheduling: profiles each model's per-batch-size latency/throughput on each GPU type (explicit, piecewise-linear size-dependent curves) and solves a bin-packing/LP problem to co-locate multiple models' batching sessions on the same GPU within a duty cycle, including duty-cycle multiplexing of an under-utilized model at small batch alongside another's batches. Explicitly multi-model, multi-GPU, cluster-scale; headline objective is cluster-wide throughput/GPU-count minimization subject to per-query SLOs for video-analytics pipelines. **Sharpest difference:** the whole design lever is spreading/packing work across many GPUs and duty-cycling multiple models onto one GPU *concurrently* — a fundamentally different resource model from our single non-preemptive accelerator with no multiplexing.

**Serving DNNs Like Clockwork: Performance Predictability from the Bottom Up.** A. Gujarati et al. OSDI 2020.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/gujarati

Centralized, fully deterministic scheduling: eliminates almost all variance sources by relying on a worker's deterministic single-request execution time (treated as effectively constant/predictable per (model, input-size), not a batch-cost curve to explore); a global scheduler assigns requests to worker/time-slots via consult-execute with explicit deadline checks, proactively rejecting what it can't meet. Multi-model, targets a cluster of many single-GPU workers; headline objective is predictability — tight, guaranteed latency SLOs and high SLO-goodput, not compute minimization. **Sharpest difference:** intentionally anti-batching-as-variance-source, scheduling against a known constant execution time across many workers — doesn't address a single accelerator choosing among heterogeneous convex/affine T_i(b) curves; its predictability goal is nearly the opposite of exploiting a measured, non-trivial batch-cost curve.

**Shepherd: Serving DNNs in the Wild.** H. Zhang et al. NSDI 2023.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi23/presentation/zhang-hong

Two-level: an offline/periodic global scheduler reserves compute-time budgets per model (batching-aware, using measured, explicitly size-dependent, often convex latency-vs-batch-size profiles) via a preemptive-priority queueing formulation, plus an online worker-local schedule packing/reordering requests within a per-model budget for deadlines and batch efficiency, aggressively multiplexing many models per GPU based on SLO "burstiness." Multi-model, multi-GPU cluster scale; headline objective is maximizing the number of SLO-satisfying models served (cluster goodput) under fixed hardware. **Sharpest difference:** the whole architecture assumes a pool of GPUs across which models are placed/multiplexed, plus preemption at request granularity between reservation rounds — never arbitrates a single non-preemptive accelerator among live competing queues at each free-up decision point the way our problem does; its "batching-awareness" feeds a placement/budgeting decision, not an online single-device batch-and-queue-selection policy.

**Symphony: Optimized Model Serving using Centralized Orchestration.** L. Chen, Y. Xin, W. Deng, A. Canumalla, A. Krishnamurthy, D. Zhuo, M. Philipose. SOSP 2023.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07470

Mechanism is "deferred batch scheduling": for each request, computes a schedulable dispatch window [frontrun, latest] from an explicitly size-dependent *affine* cost model ℓ(b) = αb + β (measured per model), and a lightweight centralized orchestrator coordinates many workers to decide, near the last possible moment, how to grow a batch before the deadline forces dispatch — near-optimal batching without a heavy global optimizer. Multi-model, cluster of many GPU workers (thousands of QPS, elastic autoscaling); headline objective is SLO-compliant throughput per dollar / minimizing GPUs needed. **Sharpest difference:** the affine-only cost model and coordination logic assume near-linear batching returns and many interchangeable workers to spread deferred batches across; doesn't confront a single fixed accelerator with heterogeneous curves where at least one model is convex, nor the discrete "which of N queues gets this one busy device next" arbitration our problem requires.

**INFaaS: A Model-less and Managed Inference Serving System.** F. Romero et al. USENIX ATC 2021.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc21/presentation/romero (preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.13348)

"Model-less" autoscaling variant selection: given a model family with many variants (architectures/precisions/batch-size-compiled versions), searches over variants and hardware to meet a query's latency/accuracy/cost requirements, then autoscales replica counts and batch sizes — per-variant batch size is a static/discrete deployment-time config, not scheduled online per queue — using measured, size-dependent latency profiles folded into variant selection. Targets a cluster of heterogeneous accelerators; headline objective is meeting SLOs at minimum resource cost. **Sharpest difference:** solves "which trained variant + how many replicas," essentially a placement/autoscaling problem; treats batch size as a per-variant static config rather than a per-decision online choice on a shared single device.

**Harpagon: Minimizing DNN Serving Cost via Efficient Dispatching, Scheduling and Splitting.** arXiv:2412.06161.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06161

Jointly optimizes request dispatching (routing among model replicas/variants), batch-aware scheduling, and model splitting (partial offload across devices), using measured, size-dependent latency curves per model-device pair in a cost-minimization ILP/heuristic. Multi-model, heterogeneous device clusters/edge-cloud collaborative setups (multiple accelerators); headline objective is minimizing total serving cost subject to latency constraints. **Sharpest difference:** the novelty is joint routing+splitting *across multiple heterogeneous devices*, presupposing a choice of *where* to run, not just when/what batch on one fixed accelerator — Harpagon's core degrees of freedom (device selection, splitting) don't exist in our single-fixed-device setting, leaving only its batch-scheduling sub-piece loosely relevant.

**Scrooge: A Cost-Effective Deep Learning Inference System.** ACM SoCC 2021.
**Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3472883.3486993

Picks, per inference *pipeline* (chains of DNN stages, e.g. video analytics), the cheapest combination of model variants and batch sizes meeting accuracy/latency targets, using measured size-dependent throughput-vs-batch profiles per stage, then packs multiple pipelines onto a cloud VM/GPU fleet to maximize resource sharing across correlated workloads. Multi-model, cluster-scale; headline objective is minimizing total cloud dollar cost while meeting per-pipeline SLOs. **Sharpest difference:** batch-size choice is made once per pipeline configuration (a provisioning-time optimization), not an online per-arrival decision on one shared non-preemptive device — no analog of a live scheduler picking among N ready queues at each accelerator free-up event.

**Proteus: A High-Throughput Inference-Serving System with Accuracy Scaling.** ASPLOS 2024.
**Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3617232.3624849

Dynamic accuracy scaling: maintains multiple accuracy-tiered variants per model and shifts traffic toward cheaper/faster variants under load, combined with variant-aware batching. Multi-model (accuracy variants of the same task), cluster of GPUs under bursty production traffic; headline objective is maximizing throughput within SLO under load spikes while preserving average accuracy. **Sharpest difference:** the key lever — trading accuracy for throughput via variant switching — is orthogonal to our problem, where each queue's model is fixed and heterogeneous by nature (not an accuracy dial); assumes a multi-GPU cluster with load-balancing across accelerators rather than one fixed device.

**Cocktail: A Multidimensional Optimization for Model Serving in Cloud.** USENIX NSDI 2022.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi22/presentation/gunasekaran (preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.05345)

Combines ensemble model selection (dynamically choosing a cost-accuracy-optimal subset of variants, refreshed periodically) with predictive autoscaling of spot/on-demand VM instances, using measured, size-dependent latency-cost profiles per variant to decide ensemble composition and batch sizes per instance type. Multi-model (ensembles over many variants), explicitly cluster/cloud-scale with heterogeneous spot-instance pools; headline objective is minimizing cloud cost while meeting latency SLOs under elastic capacity. **Sharpest difference:** the core innovation — ensemble composition plus elastic multi-instance autoscaling — has no meaning on a single fixed edge accelerator with no elasticity and no choice of *which* model serves a request (routing is fixed upstream in our problem); its batching is a secondary knob inside a much larger cloud-provisioning optimization.

**Triton Inference Server — Dynamic Batching.** NVIDIA.
**Link:** https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/triton-inference-server/user-guide/docs/user_guide/batcher.html

Per-model, per-instance queueing policy: requests accumulate and combine into a batch once either a configured preferred batch size is reached or a max-queue-delay timer expires, with optional priority levels and reject/timeout queue policies — no measured cost curve at all, effectively agnostic to whether T_i(b) is affine or convex. Supports multiple models concurrently via separate scheduler instances and concurrent model execution (time-sliced or separate GPU streams/MIG partitions); targets general GPU/CPU serving from single-GPU boxes up to clusters. **Sharpest difference:** a per-model local heuristic with no cross-queue optimization or awareness of a shared, non-preemptive single accelerator arbitrating between structurally different queues — the industrial baseline any smarter joint scheduler (ours included) should be compared against, not a competing scheduling theory.

**BATCH: Machine Learning Inference Serving on Serverless Platforms with Adaptive Batching.** A. Ali, R. Pinciroli, F. Yan, E. Smirni. IEEE/ACM SC 2020.
**Link:** https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9355312/ (PDF: https://www2.cs.uh.edu/~fyan/Paper/Feng-SC20-BATCH.pdf)

Uses queueing-theoretic (M/G/1-type) analysis of measured, explicitly size-dependent batch-latency profiles per model to compute the batch size and per-function concurrency minimizing cost under a latency SLO on serverless infrastructure, dynamically adjusting as load shifts. Effectively single-model-per-deployment; targets serverless/FaaS with elastic, ephemeral compute; headline objective is minimizing dollar cost per request under tail-latency SLO. **Sharpest difference:** assumes elastic serverless concurrency (spin up more instances) as the main lever alongside batching, and solves single-model batch sizing — no queue-selection problem across N live heterogeneous queues at all.

**MArk: Exploiting Cloud Services for Cost-Effective, SLO-Aware Machine Learning Inference Serving.** USENIX ATC 2019.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/zhang-chengliang

Combines predictive autoscaling (provisioning EC2/serverless capacity ahead of forecasted load) with dynamic batching per model instance, offloading bursty overflow to serverless as a safety valve. Largely single-model-per-service, cloud VM/serverless hybrid infrastructure; headline objective is minimizing cloud cost while bounding SLO violations during bursty load. **Sharpest difference:** central contribution is hybrid VM+serverless elastic provisioning to absorb spikes — an axis that doesn't exist on a fixed single edge accelerator with no scale-out option; batching logic is a secondary, single-model heuristic.

**MBS: Optimizing Inference Serving on Serverless Platforms.** A. Ali, R. Pinciroli, F. Yan, E. Smirni. PVLDB Vol. 15, VLDB 2022.
**Link:** https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol15/p2071-ali.pdf (DOI: https://doi.org/10.14778/3547305.3547313; code: https://github.com/Iam-ahsan/MBS)

The BATCH team's follow-on: extends the queueing-analytic approach with a more general, non-parametric (kernel-density/regression-based) technique for modeling the size-dependent, arbitrary-shape relationship between batch size and latency across diverse model architectures — improving robustness to models whose batching curves are highly non-linear/convex — inside the same cost-minimization-under-SLO framework. Remains single-model-per-function, serverless/FaaS infrastructure with elastic concurrency. **Sharpest difference:** its contribution (a better curve-fitting technique for T(b)) is the closest in spirit to our own "measured, non-constant T_i(b)" premise in this whole datacenter cluster, but it still only fits and exploits the curve for a *single* model's batch-size choice under elastic serverless scale-out — never arbitrates between multiple live queues for one non-preemptive, non-elastic accelerator, which is exactly our core scheduling problem.

## 9. LLM inference serving (adjacent, different cost structure)

**Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models.** Yu, Jeong, Kim, Kim, Chun. OSDI 2022.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi22/presentation/yu

Iteration-level (continuous) scheduling: the batch is re-formed after every single decoder iteration (one token step), so a new request can join and a finished one can leave without waiting for the whole batch to drain, paired with selective batching (batching only attention-agnostic ops so requests with different sequence lengths can share a batch). Per-job cost is emphatically not size-independent: scales with decode steps (= output length, unknown a priori) plus a prefill pass scaling with prompt length — "batch size b takes T(b)" isn't well-defined without each job's current sequence position. Jobs complete incrementally, token-by-token, and the system exploits that (early exit the moment EOS is generated) rather than atomic completion. Serves one model per deployment, doesn't mix classes in a batch (agrees with our no-class-mixing constraint). **Sharpest difference:** the "batch" itself is a mutable, per-token-refreshed set with heterogeneous progress states, whereas our batches are fixed, atomic groups of whole-image jobs of one declared size b.

**vLLM: Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention.** Kwon et al. SOSP 2023.
**Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3600006.3613165 (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)

Core mechanism is PagedAttention: treats the KV cache as non-contiguous fixed-size pages managed like OS virtual memory, decoupling logical sequence layout from physical GPU memory, packing more concurrent sequences into memory and eliminating fragmentation. Per-job cost is driven by KV-cache footprint growing linearly with sequence length, so memory pressure — not a fixed batch-size-vs-latency curve — is the binding resource; the achievable batch size is itself dynamic, shrinking/growing as sequences lengthen. Jobs complete incrementally, and paging enables fine-grained sharing (e.g. shared prompt prefixes, beam-search branches) with no analogue in atomic image inference. Single model per instance, no class mixing (matches us). **Sharpest difference:** "batch size" is not even a first-class scheduling knob — it's emergent from a paging allocator reacting to per-token memory growth, versus our fixed enumerable B_i with a measured T_i(b) curve.

**Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference with Sarathi-Serve.** Agrawal et al. OSDI 2024.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/agrawal (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02310)

Chunked-prefill + stall-free batching: a long prefill is split into fixed-size token chunks scheduled alongside ongoing decode iterations of other requests in the same iteration-level batch, smoothly interleaving prefill and decode instead of causing a decode-blocking prefill stall. Per-job cost is a direct function of prompt length (prefill chunks needed) and decode steps (unknown, incremental) — inherently non-constant per unit of "batch size." Jobs complete incrementally; batch composition is explicitly heterogeneous within one accelerator pass (a mix of prefill-chunk tokens and decode tokens from different requests simultaneously), finer-grained than even Orca's selective batching, but still one model, no cross-class mixing. **Sharpest difference:** the batch is composed of sub-token-granularity work units chosen per iteration to balance throughput/latency, whereas our batches are single, atomic, whole-job groupings of one size drawn from a fixed measured set.

**DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized Large Language Model Serving.** OSDI 2024.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/zhong-yinmin (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670)

Phase disaggregation: prefill and decode run on physically separate GPU pools, each independently batched and parallelism-tuned to its own SLO (TTFT for prefill, TPOT for decode), with KV cache transferred over the network between pools per request. Per-job cost splits into two differently-shaped functions — prefill compute-bound scaling with prompt length, decode memory-bandwidth-bound scaling with generated-token count and growing KV-cache — the opposite of a single T_i(b) curve. Jobs complete incrementally and a single logical job executes non-atomically across two physical pools, with no equivalent in our single-accelerator, single-pass model. One model per deployment, no class mixing. **Sharpest difference:** the entire design assumes multi-accelerator disaggregation is available and beneficial, whereas our problem is explicitly a single compute-constrained accelerator where such spatial splitting isn't an option.

**Splitwise: Efficient Generative LLM Inference Using Phase Splitting.** ISCA 2024.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18677

Near-identical in spirit to DistServe: separate machine pools for compute-heavy prefill and memory-bandwidth-heavy decode, with a cluster-level scheduler routing requests across pools (can use cheaper/lower-power hardware for decode), plus mixed-batching within the decode pool to keep GPUs saturated. Per-job cost again bifurcates into one-shot prefill and incremental per-token decode cost; batch composition on the decode side is continuously reshaped. Single model per deployment, no cross-class mixing. **Sharpest difference:** optimization target is datacenter-scale hardware heterogeneity and power/cost efficiency across many accelerators — the reverse of our single edge accelerator where "which pool" isn't a decision that exists.

**Mooncake: A KVCache-centric Disaggregated Architecture for LLM Serving.** arXiv:2407.00079.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00079

KV-cache pool disaggregated from compute: prefill/decode instances separate, a global scheduler proactively moves/reuses KV-cache blocks across a distributed CPU/DRAM/SSD cache pool to maximize cache-hit-driven prefill savings, with prediction-based early rejection to keep decode SLOs under overload. Per-job cost is dominated by KV-cache size and hit rate — a cache hit can skip most of prefill, so cost for "the same job" varies with system history/cache state, a statefulness with no analogue in a T_i(b) lookup table. Jobs complete incrementally, non-atomically across disaggregated, geographically-distinct components. Single model class, no batch mixing. **Sharpest difference:** the whole value proposition is a large, distributed, reusable KV-cache substrate spanning many machines — the polar opposite of a single edge accelerator with no cross-request cache reuse in our whole-image-inference setting.

**S-LoRA: Serving Thousands of Concurrent LoRA Adapters.** MLSys 2024.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03285

Unified paging (extending PagedAttention-style paging to adapter weights, not just KV cache) plus a custom batched-GEMM kernel that computes the base-model matmul once and applies many different low-rank adapter updates to different rows of the same batch in one fused kernel call. Per-job cost depends on sequence length as usual, layered with adapter-swap/load overhead. Jobs complete incrementally. **Critically**, S-LoRA explicitly batches requests targeting *different LoRA adapters* (effectively different fine-tuned model variants) together in a single forward pass — a direct violation of our one-queue-per-batch, no-class-mixing constraint, and the paper's central contribution. **Sharpest difference:** our problem forbids exactly what S-LoRA is built to do — its whole point is efficiently co-batching heterogeneous "model classes" that share a frozen base, whereas our models are fully independent with no shared parameters and must never share a batch.

**Punica: Multi-Tenant LoRA Serving.** MLSys 2024.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18547

Segmented Gather Matrix-Vector multiplication (SGMV) CUDA kernel: batches the shared base-model computation across requests while gathering and applying each request's distinct LoRA delta in the same kernel launch, plus a scheduler consolidating multi-tenant traffic onto few active GPUs and migrating adapters to improve utilization. Per-job cost tracks sequence length/decode-step count plus adapter-application overhead SGMV amortizes to near-zero. Jobs complete incrementally. Like S-LoRA, the entire premise is mixing many different LoRA "tenants" into one batch and kernel call — the direct opposite of our incompatible-classes, one-queue-per-batch rule. **Sharpest difference:** treats "different model" as a cheap, mergeable delta on a shared frozen backbone that can share a batch; our models are structurally unrelated (different architectures/weights) and can never share a batch, and it targets multi-GPU tenant consolidation rather than a single constrained edge device.

**dLoRA: Dynamically Orchestrating Requests and Adapters for LoRA LLM Serving.** OSDI 2024.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/wu-bingyang

Dynamic adapter-request orchestration: decides at runtime whether to merge a LoRA adapter into base weights (cheaper compute, requires unmerging to switch) or keep it unmerged (batchable across adapters via SGMV-like kernels with per-token overhead), and dynamically migrates adapters/requests across worker replicas to balance load — a merge/unmerge decision with no counterpart in our fixed-B_i batching problem. Per-job cost depends on prompt length/decode steps plus a merge-state-dependent term. Jobs complete incrementally. Like S-LoRA/Punica, explicitly batches requests for different LoRA adapters together in the unmerged regime — another direct instance of intentional cross-class batch mixing our model forbids. **Sharpest difference:** the scheduling problem is fundamentally about a merge/batch tradeoff for parameter-efficient variants of one base model across a multi-replica cluster, whereas ours is single-accelerator timing/batching decisions for structurally independent, non-mergeable models.

**AlpaServe: Statistical Multiplexing with Model Parallelism for Deep Learning Serving.** OSDI 2023.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11665

Closest in spirit to our problem among this whole cluster: serves multiple *distinct, independent* DL models (not LoRA variants of one base) on a shared GPU cluster via model-parallel placement plus statistical multiplexing — deciding how to partition/replicate each model across devices and route requests to exploit that different models' load bursts rarely peak simultaneously, so idle capacity from one can serve another's burst. Predates the LLM-serving line, largely targets classic (non-autoregressive-decode) DNN inference, so per-job cost is closer to our setting — a roughly fixed cost per model/request, atomic completion. Does NOT batch different models' requests together in one forward pass — multiplexes them in time/space across devices/parallelism groups, respecting a mixing constraint much closer to ours than the LoRA papers. **Sharpest difference:** optimizes model *placement and parallelization* across a multi-GPU cluster to exploit cross-model burstiness — a fundamentally different lever than our single fixed accelerator with only per-queue batch-timing decisions available.

**MuxServe: Flexible Spatial-Temporal Multiplexing for Multiple LLM Serving.** ICML 2024.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02015

Spatial-temporal multiplexing of multiple independent LLMs on shared GPUs: colocates multiple full LLMs' prefill and decode stages on the same devices, using temporal multiplexing (interleaving different models' iterations over time, exploiting that prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound) and spatial partitioning to raise utilization beyond single-model serving. Per-job cost follows the standard LLM shape, stacked across several distinct model classes sharing hardware simultaneously. Jobs complete incrementally. **Critically**, explicitly interleaves/colocates different LLM *classes'* execution on the same accelerator at the same time — while not literally batching different classes' tokens into one matmul the way LoRA papers do, it's still a direct violation of "a batch contains jobs from one queue only, run non-preemptively to completion," since the whole point is concurrent, interleaved execution of different model classes on shared hardware. **Sharpest difference:** assumes enough hardware/memory to hold several distinct large models resident and interleaved concurrently, whereas our compute-constrained edge accelerator runs one batch of one model class at a time, non-preemptively, with no cross-model concurrency.

**Fairness in Serving Large Language Models (Virtual Token Counter / VTC).** arXiv:2401.00588.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00588

A fair-queuing scheduler adapted from network fair-queuing (weighted fair queuing) to LLM decoding: tracks a per-client "virtual token counter" of service received (input + output tokens, weighted differently), greedily services the client with least counted service next, with a bounded-unfairness proof analogous to classic fair-queuing bounds. Cost is measured in tokens processed, tying priority directly to the incremental, KV-cache-driven cost structure of decoding rather than a per-request count. Jobs complete incrementally, fairness accounting updates continuously. Serves one model, no cross-class batch mixing — orthogonal contribution (a fairness policy layered on top of whatever batching mechanism is used). **Sharpest difference:** the core insight — cost must be measured in a fine-grained, incrementally-accruing unit (tokens) rather than per-job — has no analogue in our setting, where a whole-image job has one atomic, upfront cost T_i(b); the fairness objective (per-client service equity) also differs from our throughput/turnaround objective.

**Llumnix: Dynamic Scheduling for Large Language Model Serving.** OSDI 2024.
**Link:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/sun-biao (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03243)

Live request migration across a multi-instance LLM-serving cluster: continuously re-schedules already-running requests by migrating their KV cache between GPU instances (an efficient live-migration protocol overlapping migration with ongoing generation) to de-fragment memory, isolate long-tail requests, and rebalance load — essentially process migration for LLM decode state, which requires jobs to be preemptible/relocatable mid-flight. Per-job cost driven by decode-step count and KV-cache size; a job's state can be relocated without restarting, presupposing exactly the divisible, resumable, incremental execution our atomic whole-image jobs don't have. Single model per cluster, no cross-class mixing. **Sharpest difference:** the core mechanism (migrating a live, partially-completed job between accelerators) is only possible because LLM jobs decompose into resumable token steps; our jobs are atomic and non-preemptive on a single accelerator, so there's no state to migrate and no multi-instance rebalancing decision to make.

**QLM: Queue Management for SLO-Oriented Large Language Model Serving.** ACM SoCC 2024.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00047

A request-level queue-management layer built on Markov-chain-based estimation of expected output length/latency per request, used to compute queuing policies — pulling/eviction order, batch composition, model-instance/swap decisions across multiple backends — solved periodically to jointly meet multiple SLOs across heterogeneous request classes sharing infrastructure. Per-job cost estimated via a predicted output-length distribution (true decode-step count unknown a priori) and re-estimated as generation proceeds — a genuinely stochastic, incrementally-revised cost model unlike our measured, deterministic T_i(b). Jobs complete incrementally; QLM operates above/around whatever batching mechanism (e.g. continuous batching) the backend uses. Targets multiple request classes/SLO tiers sharing common backends, but its "classes" are SLO/priority tiers of requests to the same or a small set of models, not incompatible model architectures — doesn't batch different model classes into one physical batch. **Sharpest difference:** scheduling decisions are built on probabilistic, continuously-updated length predictions for incrementally-completing jobs across a multi-instance backend, whereas our problem has deterministic, pre-measured per-batch-size costs for atomic jobs on one fixed accelerator with no prediction uncertainty to manage.

## 10. Energy and DVFS for inference

**Coordinated Batching and DVFS for DNN Inference on GPU Accelerators.** S. M. Nabavinejad, S. Reda, M. Ebrahimi. IEEE Transactions on Parallel and Distributed Systems 33(10):2496-2508, 2022, DOI 10.1109/TPDS.2022.3144614.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1109/TPDS.2022.3144614 (IEEE Xplore: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9689937/)

Tunes batch size and GPU core frequency *together* rather than as separable problems, extending the same authors' earlier batch-size-only work (BatchSizer, ASPDAC'21, a pseudo-binary-search heuristic on a Tesla P40 with up to 29% throughput gain over plain DVFS). **Note:** the exact headline percentage and whether the joint search here is heuristic, exhaustive, or model-based sit behind an IEEE paywall that couldn't be resolved during this pass (corroborated only indirectly via two citing papers' related-work text) — flagged rather than guessed. Targets combined batch-size + core-frequency space for GPU-accelerated DNN inference on server GPUs, minimizing energy/latency trade-offs. **Sharpest relevance:** closest in spirit to our own problem of the three energy papers — if DVFS became an objective, T_i(b) would need to become T_i(b,f), and their coordinated search strategy over (b,f) is a natural template for extending our batch-selection logic without a full state-space blowup.

**Camel: Energy-Aware LLM Inference on Resource-Constrained Devices.** arXiv:2508.09173.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.09173

Jointly tunes GPU frequency and batch size for LLM inference on edge hardware, using an exploration-exploitation configuration search to find good (batch, frequency) operating points rather than a closed-form model. Validated on a Jetson AGX Orin — the same device class as our target — reporting 12.4%-29.9% reduction in energy-delay product versus default configurations. The most architecturally similar of the energy papers to our setting: single edge accelerator, batching as a first-class knob, empirical validation. **Sharpest relevance:** the most direct template if energy becomes an objective — T_i(b) becomes T_i(b,f), and its exploration-exploitation search over the joint (batch, frequency) space could replace or augment a pure batch-size dispatcher with minimal architectural change, since it already assumes one accelerator serving batched requests non-preemptively.

**DVFS-Aware DNN Inference on GPUs: Latency Modeling and Performance Analysis.** arXiv:2502.06295.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06295

Tunes GPU frequency only (no batch-size dimension), closed-form analytical rather than search/learning: derives t_n = a_n·f^(-b_n) + c_n, an inference-time-vs-frequency model fit per DNN/workload/device, correcting prior CPU-DVFS-derived models that underestimate GPU inference time at high frequencies (where memory bandwidth, not compute, bottlenecks). Validated on Jetson Xavier NX and Orin Nano (plus RTX 4080 comparison), reporting 66%/69% reductions in inference time/energy versus the CPU-DVFS baseline. **Sharpest relevance:** the cleanest fit for turning T_i(b) into T_i(b,f) analytically rather than empirically — their closed-form per-model latency-vs-frequency curve could be measured once per (model, batch size) pair and plugged directly into our existing measured-curve framework.

## 11. Measurement and profiling

**Adaptive Scheduling for Edge-Assisted DNN Serving.** arXiv:2304.09961.
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09961

No DVFS or frequency tuning — purely a batching/scheduling paper, closest to our problem's non-DVFS half. Schedules multiple DNN request queues (including shared-layer multi-DNN batching) onto a server GPU using a dynamic-programming algorithm that splits request sequences into segments to minimize completion time or maximize on-time delivery ratio, plus an option for collaborative client-side local processing. Validated on a Tesla P100 server GPU paired with a Jetson Nano client over WiFi/LTE traces, across VGG16/ResNet50/GoogleNet/SSD/FCN/SDCNet/RTA, under Poisson/Pareto/constant arrivals — reporting 20%-67% capacity improvement over plain batching (up to 400% over no-batching) for single-DNN, and 25%-32% completion-time reduction over Nexus for multi-DNN workloads. **Sharpest relevance:** a pure-scheduling comparison point (multi-queue, per-model batching, deadline-aware objectives) with no energy dimension — validates the batching-DP approach itself rather than offering a DVFS extension path; would only become relevant to an energy objective if its segment-splitting DP were borrowed as the batching-decision core with a frequency dimension bolted on separately.

## Caveats

- **Paywalled OR papers**: Chen & Wang (2022)'s finite-capacity extension could not be confirmed as a full optimality proof versus just "a generalization of the policy" — both the ScienceDirect and SSRN full texts were inaccessible during this pass. Verify before leaning on that claim.
- **Two citation corrections found this pass**: the "polling systems with set-up costs" reference previously attributed to Koole (Queueing Systems, 1998, DOI 10.1007/BF01151932) actually resolves to **Duenyas & Van Oyen (1995)** under that DOI — corrected above. The "Ansell, Glazebrook & Niño-Mora" Whittle-index attribution should read **Glazebrook, Lumley & Ansell (2003)** — Niño-Mora's co-authorship on that specific paper could not be confirmed.
- **EdgeServing (arXiv 2605.05527) and other 2026 arXiv items are recent preprints**, not confirmed peer-reviewed. The Pith review of EdgeServing explicitly flags the stability score's predictive accuracy as unproven.
- **One IEEE-paywalled empirical claim left unverified**: Nabavinejad, Reda & Ebrahimi (2022)'s exact headline number and whether their (b,f) search is heuristic/exhaustive/model-based couldn't be pulled past the paywall this pass — corroborated as real and on-topic via two independent citing papers, but the precise result is unconfirmed.
- **The PERTINENCE "36% fewer operations" headline is contested**: an independent review (Pith) notes the genetic algorithm is fit on the test set while final numbers are reported on a validation set, so the efficiency claim may be optimistic. Don't lean on that figure when motivating this project's extension. (PERTINENCE: Shende, Ananthanarayanan & Traiola, arXiv 2507.01695.)
- **Our arrival process (periodic, routed by the dispatcher) differs from the Poisson arrivals most OR results above assume.** Whether LQ/CAW optimality carries over to deterministic-periodic arrivals with fixed routing isn't established in any of the papers above — worth checking empirically in `scheduler-sim` rather than assuming it transfers.
